How it works

What happens inside your battery when you plug it in

Four parts, one tiny traveller, and the reason batteries wear out. Swipe →

The four parts

Every lithium-ion cell has the same four layers

Cathode (+)

Metal oxide or phosphate. Holds the lithium when the battery is empty.

Anode (−)

Usually graphite. Holds the lithium when the battery is full.

Electrolyte

A lithium salt in solvent. The road the ions travel on.

Separator

A porous plastic film that keeps + and − from touching.

Plugged in

Charging pushes lithium ions from the cathode to the anode

Electrons take the outside route, through the charger and wires, to meet them there.

Using your phone

Unplugged, the ions flow back to the cathode.

The electrons go back through your device on the way, and that flow is the power you're using.

Battery University: "charge reverses the direction and the ions flow from the cathode to the anode."

The unsung hero

The separator is about a third of a hair thick

A common one is about 25 µm thick, and some are now down to 12 µm. Its pores let ions through but block electrons.

Many separators have a built-in fuse: if the cell overheats, a polyethylene layer melts at about 130°C (266°F) and closes its pores.

Why batteries wear out

Every trip costs a little lithium

Side reactions with heat and high voltage slowly trap lithium so it can't travel any more. Less moving lithium means less capacity.

Charging below freezing is worse: lithium can plate onto the anode as metal, which is permanent.

Recap

Charging moves ions + → −. Using it moves them − → +. Heat and cold wear it out.
